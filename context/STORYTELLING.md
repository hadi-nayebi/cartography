# Stories that unfold through city data

A chart should carry a developing story, not merely decorate a sequence of
slides. Start with the viewer's question, preserve an effective existing visual
structure, and add understanding at the moment it becomes useful.

## The spine of a temporal story

1. **Open a question.** Give the viewer a reason to follow the change: a striking
   but honest difference, a local pattern or a surprising contrast.
2. **Let the measure unfold.** Keep the year, units and chart stable enough to
   follow. Progressive bars can reveal a long arc; the animation must expose
   data rather than merely move decoration.
3. **Locate the era in memory.** Add a few recognizable, verified calendar
   landmarks as the chart reaches their years: the Web, a familiar technology,
   a cultural event or a local milestone. These answer “what time was that?”
   without claiming to explain the trend.
4. **Develop the data story.** Interleave those landmarks with findings that
   explain shape, composition or scale. A useful annotation adds meaning beyond
   repeating the large number already on screen.
5. **Change scale for a reason.** Move from years to months, or from a citywide
   histogram to an active map, when it answers the next question. The map must
   reveal location, distribution or change; it is not an ornamental sidebar.
6. **Deliver a payoff.** Return to the opening question with two or three things
   the viewer can now explain. Leave source and reproduction details available.

## Two kinds of annotation

A data finding is supported by a calculation on the exact plotted data. A
calendar landmark is supported by a dated source and helps the viewer place the
story in time. Neither automatically establishes a causal relationship.

For example, “2005: YouTube's first video goes online” can accompany the crime
histogram reaching 2005. It does not require a lecture about causal inference
on screen. Do not connect the event to the trend with causal words or arrows
unless separate evidence supports that claim. Keep the scholarly distinction
in the source record and make the viewer-facing sentence natural and factual.

Record each cue's date, text, source, role, start time and reading window.
Avoid overlapping notes. A short landmark may need six seconds; a substantive
finding may need eight or more. Test the actual timing, not only word counts.

## Viewer copy and map purpose

Write about the city, events and evidence. “BPD introduced a broader incident
record in 2015” explains a real change. “Do not join these data points” instructs
a producer and does not belong in the film. Give a reporting break truthful
visual treatment and concise factual language.

For a snapshot story, the progression can be spatial rather than historical:
city to borough to neighborhood to a concrete place, then back to the whole.
Do not invent a temporal series because the framework has a timeline example.

## Improve an existing film

Inspect the actual previous render before editing. Name what already works and
retain it unless there is a specific reason to replace it. Compare revised
encoded samples against that baseline for narrative progression, useful detail,
active geographic information, reading time and clarity. Simpler is not by
itself better. A passing render, attractive still or confident score is not
proof of improvement; human calibration remains part of the work.

Retain each revision's source and bytes, apply concrete feedback, and carry the
resulting correction into the next production. Keep rejected work distinct
from accepted or published work.

## Editorial selection and retention

Choose anchors for the actual city, topic and chart. Both broadly recognizable
cultural milestones and local events can belong. Judge the contribution of each;
there is no mandatory list and no blanket ban on technology or cultural events.
A suggestion is an example,
not a template to repeat. Record why each event belongs here and what the viewer
learns from it. Prefer a directly relevant local event or clear data finding over
an unrelated familiar date. Investigate plausible explanations for a spike;
use causal language only when evidence supports it. Omit weak anchors.

Earn attention with an early truthful payoff, readable motion and new information
at each stage. Inspect the opening seconds, chart development and geographic
payoff rather than adding arbitrary animation. Retention is measured after
release; full retention and a winning thumbnail cannot be promised in advance.

## Official platform guidance to apply

[YouTube’s title/thumbnail guidance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12340300?hl=en)
supports accurate, concise wording. Put the city/topic first and make the promise
visible at small thumbnail size. [The retention guide](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415)
uses the first 30 seconds to assess the introduction. Check whether that opening
fulfills the packaging promise; after release investigate actual dips and spikes.
Do not treat arbitrary animation frequency or a generic retention percentage as
a universal rule for educational maps.
